🧭 This page describes the research areas in which I am actively looking to supervise doctoral projects. Please read it before writing to me about opportunities for supervision: it sets out the three strands of my research programme and the proposals that I am most likely to consider. Each strand has a *subject tag*; when you approach me, use it in your email's subject line (details in [[Study with me|Study with me]]).

My research is organised across three general strands or themes. They overlap, meaning projects often span two or even all three. I expect doctoral projects to sit in one of these. The sample research questions under each are not prescriptive, only meant as calibration for your own thinking and interests — take one, invert it, or use it to test whether your proposed project idea lives in this neighbourhood. The common thread to all of them is Phil Agre's notion of ["critical technical practice"](https://pages.gseis.ucla.edu/faculty/agre/critical.html). For a broader picture of my research, see [[Research]].

## AI interfaces and media innovation

**Subject tag:** `creative-ai`

**Human-computer interaction & design:** new computational media formats and aesthetics, modalities of interaction with AI systems. I'm interested in design ideas that require the reconceptualisation of human tasks and activities, individually and collectively, beyond prompting a chatbot. This can include novel annotation practices and AI training regimes, edge and small footprint computing, federated learning and distributed inference, interfaces and media for rapid prototyping, new GUIs, hyperspecialist models, and similar ideas in the lineage of what used to be called cybernetics. The common thread here is ideas for systems that take model plus interface plus institution, together, as the designable space for creative AI.

**Sample research questions**

1. What forms of creative control do generative video systems need *beyond* natural language — and what interaction paradigms (cinematographic steering, parametric storyboarding) make machine learning craftable rather than merely promptable?
2. As generative models enter film/TV/music-video pipelines, where do creative decisions migrate — what new roles, crafts, and gatekeeping practices appear at the prompt–edit boundary?
3. Can LLM-driven characters sustain dramatic interaction? What authoring and control structures would turn improvised machine performance into a designable medium — in games, interactive drama, or live media?
4. Can the computational supercut mature from experiment to format — what can parametric montage do that manual editing cannot?

**Resources:** [[Projects/Made by Machine|Made by Machine]], [[Projects/Creanalytics|Creanalytics]], [[Notes/Motion Canvas|Motion Canvas]]. Interactionist AI is an approach that views intelligence as emerging from an agent's continuous, real-world interaction with its environment rather than from isolated internal reasoning. Agre articulated this framework in his 1997 book, [Computation and Human Experience](https://dl.acm.org/doi/book/10.5555/522884), as a direct critique of classical "mentalist" AI. Also have a look at [Why Code Is More Important Than Flat Design](https://direct.mit.edu/books/oa-monograph/5814/chapter/5012150/Why-Code-Is-More-Important-Than-Flat-Design) and [When the Interface Flattens Into a Prompt](https://youtu.be/ccT0jjd36I4?si=6o5utKcmx9RD1U0Z).

## Sculpting time with computers

**Subject tag:** `computational-modelling`

**Modelling and analysis:** computational approaches to **time-based media** — moving image & sound primarily, but also digital and interactive works where temporal form is essential to the medium.

This strand connects theoretical debates in the philosophy of time with concrete data representations, ontologies, and computational modelling techniques. I am specifically interested in perdurantism (four-dimensionalism), temporal parts, and hierarchical emergence, and their implications for computational modelling and simulation of screen culture. This strand fits project ideas related to cultural analytics, distant viewing, machine spectatorship, and similar approaches — including large-N accounts of style drift over decades and platforms, or dynamic system approaches through agents or gameplay — as long as they are situated and take temporal form as a complex phenomenon to be modelled (sculpted).

**Sample research questions**

1. What do video-understanding models encode and fail to encode of cinematic time — duration, rhythm, continuity — and can film-theoretical concepts be operationalised into new encodings?
2. What is the evidential status of machine-generated archival description? Can enrichment at scale be evaluated in terms that archival practice itself accepts — and what do AI-generated records do to screen archive cultures?
3. Can temporal representations travel across time-based media — e.g. is semantic segmentation incommensurable between moving images and sound? What would shared temporal representations look like?
4. Can agent-based models of screen industries — production networks, gatekeeping, attention economies — reproduce observed dynamics and generate testable hypotheses, for instance about the effects of synthetic content on discovery and valuation?

**Resources:** [[Events/Sculpting Time with Computers/Sculpting Time With Computers|Sculpting Time with Computers]], [[Cinema and Machine Vision|Cinema and Machine Vision]], [[Projects/ISSA|ISSA]], [[Projects/FrameSense|FrameSense]], [[Projects/Focal length classifier|Focal length classifier]], [[Notes/Videogrep|Videogrep]]. See also: [How Things Persist](https://academic.oup.com/book/5220), [Out of Time: A Philosophical Study of Timelessness](https://academic.oup.com/book/41491), and [Time Blindness: Why Video-Language Models Can't See What Humans Can](https://timeblindness.github.io/).

## Media for AI literacy

**Subject tag:** `ai-literacy`

**Narratives and mythologies of AI:** how the technology is thematised, imagined, and argued about through media — the rhetorical techniques used to drive its deployment, adoption or rejection by specific audiences. In particular, I am interested in developing better public definitions, explanations and visual representations of concepts such as high-dimensionality, embeddings, semantic geometry, stochastic processes, network effects and scaling laws. This strand involves designing media that builds public AI literacy, from metaphors to visual representations to playable formats. This is currently the smallest part of my portfolio and one that I am hoping to grow.

**Sample research questions**

1. Which mythologies of AI — the synthetic mind, the autonomous agent, superintelligence, the existential threat — structure public understanding, and how do their iconographies recur and mutate across screen fiction, news, and platform media?
2. How do galleries, libraries, archives, and museums narrate machine intelligence in exhibitions and learning programmes — and what do audiences actually take away?
3. Can playable media teach the social and cultural dynamics of an emergent technology — what can non-specialists learn from *enacting* a technological system that reading/viewing about it cannot teach?
4. What does hands-on engagement with small open models — running, fine-tuning, breaking open — teach that descriptive and declarative accounts cannot, and what core capacities (applied knowledge and skills) does it build for critically interrogating AI systems?

**Resources:** see [AI Narratives: A History of Imaginative Thinking about Intelligent Machines](https://academic.oup.com/book/36637), [AILit Framework](https://ailiteracyframework.org/), [AI Literacies framework for public service media](https://downloads.bbc.co.uk/rd/responsible-innovation/responsible-innovation-centre-ai-literacies-young-adults.pdf), [AI: the growing UK pushback](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/380083322/DFI_AI_Pushback_Report.pdf), [AI in the Movies](https://edinburghuniversitypress.com/book-ai-in-the-movies.html).

## How to know your project fits

I am drawn to proposals that do all five:

1. **A specific object of study** — a collection, corpus, platform, industry or apparatus, not "AI in general".
2. **A question individual disciplines cannot answer alone** — where it is clear what each discipline brings to the table and how the combination is more than the sum of their respective knowledges.
3. **A technical component you can personally run** — analysis, dataset-building, tooling, production experiments.
4. **Data and access you can actually get** — where the material comes from, what rights are involved: can you realistically get it, or how can I help you to get it?
5. **Engagement with my work** — point to one specific piece of mine (paper, chapter, tool, project) and say how you build on or depart from it.

## What I can't supervise

- Film/TV/media theory and history with **no computational component** → better colleagues in Film Studies and CMCI.
- **Pure ML/Computer Vision engineering** without a genuine humanities research question → belongs in a CS department.
- **Text-only corpus projects** (literary/historical distant reading, no visual or audiovisual dimension).
- Broad **"AI and society"** projects that engage neither screen culture specifically nor technical detail.
- Projects whose primary aim is **building a product, service or venture**.

I will consider projects adjacent to the themes above and not only on moving images, as long as an appropriate co-supervisor can be identified.
